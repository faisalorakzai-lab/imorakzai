# Research Notes: Page 80 — Understanding History Through Multiple Sources

## Core Concept
- **Methodological Triangulation**: The practice of using multiple data sources, methods, and perspectives to reconstruct a more complete and reliable historical narrative.
- **Independence vs. Multiplicity**: Distinguishing between having "many sources" and "many independent sources." Redundant sources (those copying from the same original) do not provide independent corroboration.
- **Conflict as Evidence**: Recognizing that disagreements between sources (dates, names, interpretations) are not failures but data points revealing different perspectives, memory shifts, or administrative biases.

## Key Findings

### 1. Triangulation Strategies
- **Data Triangulation**: Comparing evidence across time, space, and persons (e.g., comparing a 19th-century colonial report with a 21st-century oral history).
- **Methodological Triangulation**: Using different research methods (e.g., archival research, ethnography, cartographic analysis) to study the same event or tradition.
- **Source**: *Methodological Triangulation* (PMC9714985); *Triangulation in Research* (Scribbr).

### 2. Orakzai Case Study Examples
- **Origin Legends**: The story of "Sikandar Shah" (the lost son) exists as a shared oral tradition across Orakzai clans. While legendary, its persistence across independent family lineages and its recording in colonial dictionaries (1910) provide a triangulated view of tribal identity formation.
- **Sectarian Dynamics (1979)**: The pivotal events of 1979 in Kurram and Orakzai can be reconstructed by triangulating official government security reports, contemporary newspaper accounts, and retrospective oral histories from women and elders in the affected communities.
- **Traditional Food & Ethnobotany**: Recent studies in *Nature* (2026) on wild plant use in Orakzai can be triangulated with traditional recipes and seasonal migration patterns (transhumance) documented in older ethnographic works like Akbar S. Ahmed's.
- **Source**: *Alisherzai clan* (Facebook/Barmazid); *Nature* (s41598-026-57524-z); *EBSCO* (Early Warning by Women).

### 3. Handling Contradictions
- **Dates & Calendars**: Discrepancies often arise from the use of different systems (Hijri vs. Gregorian) or the gap between an event occurring and it being reported in an annual administration report.
- **Names & Spellings**: Multiple spellings (e.g., Orakzai, Orkzai, Wurukzai) reveal transliteration shifts from Pashto to Persian/Urdu to English, rather than necessarily indicating different groups.
- **Source**: *Dating Muslim traditions* (JSTOR 4057795).

### 4. Scale and Scope
- **Geographic Scale**: Evidence from one village (e.g., a specific marriage custom) should not be expanded to describe the entire Orakzai tribe without corroboration from other valleys.
- **Temporal Scale**: A single reported incident of resistance in 1897 does not prove a permanent state of hostility; it must be contextualized within long-term political negotiation.

## Evidence Hierarchy
- **Convergent Evidence**: Claims supported by Archive + Map + Oral History + Material Trace (Highest Confidence).
- **Complementary Evidence**: Sources that answer different parts of the same question (e.g., Archive gives the date, Oral History gives the experience).
- **Dissonant Evidence**: Sources that contradict each other, requiring investigation into bias or reporting gaps.

## Confidence Labels
- **High Confidence**: Convergent independent sources.
- **Medium Confidence**: Complementary sources with minor gaps.
- **Low Confidence**: Single uncorroborated source or conflicting independent accounts without resolution.
- **Insufficient Evidence**: Contradictory sources with no clear explanation or missing primary data.
