# Research Notes: Page 75 — Tribal Resistance

## Core Concept
- **Resistance as Response**: Tribal resistance in the Orakzai context is understood as a response to external pressure on local autonomy, land, and customary authority.
- **Multifaceted Resistance**: It encompasses armed conflict, political refusal, non-cooperation, and negotiation, rather than being a state of "permanent war."
- **Clanal Diversity**: Resistance was rarely a unified tribal act; it was often section-specific, village-specific, or temporary, reflecting the complex internal politics of the Orakzai.

## Key Findings

### 1. Defining Resistance
- **Colonial Categories**: Terms like "fanaticism," "lawlessness," and "rebellion" were administrative labels used to categorize tribal behavior that challenged state paramountcy.
- **Historical Forms**:
    - **Armed Resistance**: Lashkars, sieges (e.g., Samana posts, Saragarhi), and guerilla warfare.
    - **Political Refusal**: Refusing to attend jirgas called by the Political Agent, withholding information about offenders.
    - **Non-Cooperation**: Refusal to pay fines (*chatti*), refusal to accept colonial infrastructure (roads, signal posts).
    - **Social/Territorial**: Cross-border movement to avoid administrative control, defending grazing lands.

### 2. Major Resistance Events (Orakzai-Specific)
- **1855 (Bizoti Section)**: Resistance following border raids; led to the first major punitive expedition under Neville Chamberlain.
- **1868-69 (Bizoti & Utman Khel)**: Armed resistance against colonial border enforcement; documented as a period of "protracted resistance."
- **1891 (Miranzai Resistance)**: Orakzai clans on the Samana Range resisted the construction of Fort Lockhart and Fort Gulistan.
- **1897-98 (Great Frontier Uprising)**: Coordinated resistance involving Mamozai, Alikhel, Mishti, and Alisherzai sections. Orakzai lashkars participated in the siege of the Samana posts and the Battle of Saragarhi.
- **Source**: *The Army in India and Mountain Warfare* (Lockhart, 1898); *Edge of Empire* (Tripodi, 2013).

### 3. Factors Driving Resistance
- **Autonomy**: Protection of the *Jirga* and customary law against the *Frontier Crimes Regulation (FCR)*.
- **Land**: Resistance to colonial mapping and road-building which was seen as a precursor to taxation and control.
- **Identity/Religion**: Influence of religious leaders (e.g., Hadda Mulla) who framed resistance as a defense of faith.
- **Economic**: Disruption of traditional trade and grazing rights.

### 4. Resistance vs. Negotiation
- **The Negotiated Balance**: Resistance and negotiation were two sides of the same coin. A clan might fight one year and negotiate an allowance (*muajib*) the next.
- **Patronage**: The Malik system was used by the British to create a pro-government faction, often leading to internal tribal divisions between those who negotiated and those who resisted.

## Evidence Hierarchy
- **A. Historical Records**: British military despatches, Parliamentary papers (1850-1898), Political Agent reports.
- **B. Academic Scholarship**: Tripodi (2013) on tribal administration; S. Arbab (2019) on Pashtun resistance movements.
- **C. Oral History**: Tribal memories of the "Samana wars" and the 1897 uprising (to be treated as testimony).

## Source Criticism
- **Bias Check**: Colonial sources (e.g., Hutchinson, 1898) often omit the internal tribal grievances that triggered resistance, focusing instead on "tribal character."
- **Precision**: Identifying specific sections (e.g., "the Bizoti Orakzais") rather than "the Orakzai" to avoid historical over-generalization.
