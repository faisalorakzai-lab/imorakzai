# Research Notes: Page 71 — Orakzai Before British Rule

## Core Concept
- **Autonomy and Frontier Dynamics**: Before British rule (pre-1849), Orakzai society was characterized by tribal autonomy, negotiated relationships with neighboring empires (Mughal, Durrani, Sikh), and a strong internal structure based on kinship and customary law.
- **Historical Timeline**:
    - **Pre-Modern**: Tribal emergence and oral traditions.
    - **Mughal Period (16th-17th C)**: Resistance under the Roshaniyya movement; strategic importance of the frontier.
    - **Durrani Period (1747-early 19th C)**: Part of the Afghan/Pashtun confederation; negotiated sovereignty.
    - **Sikh Expansion (early 19th C)**: Confrontation and frontier forts under the Sikh Empire.
    - **British Expansion (1849 onwards)**: The transition to formal colonial administration.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Roshaniyya Movement and Mughal Resistance
- **Pir Roshan (Bayazid Ansari)**: The Orakzai and Afridi tribes were core supporters of the 16th-century Roshaniyya movement, which resisted Mughal authority under Akbar and Jahangir.
- **Tirah as Refuge**: The high valleys of Tirah served as a strategic refuge for the Roshaniyya heretics and tribal rebels.
- **Mughal Chronicles**: The *Akbarnama* and *Ain-i-Akbari* document military campaigns against the Orakzai, who were described as fierce and independent.
- **Source**: *Khyber Pass in Imperial Politics of the Mughals* (2013); *The Savage Border* (J. Stewart, 2007).

### 2. Tribal Organization and Clans
- **Lucas White King (1900)**: His monograph "The Orakzai Country and Clans" provides the definitive historical breakdown of the tribe into 18 clans, including major sections like the Alisherzai, Mamozai, and Mishti.
- **Genealogy**: Orakzai oral tradition traces their origin to a "lost son" (Orakzai meaning "the lost son"), a legend that serves as a powerful identity marker even if not documentary fact.
- **Source**: *Monograph on the Orakzai Country and Clans* (L. White King, 1900).

### 3. Religious Authority and Sectarian Diversity
- **Tirah Sayyids**: The influence of the Tirah Sayyids led to some Orakzai sub-sections (like the Mani Khel and Sipah) adopting the Shia faith, creating a unique religious landscape in the region.
- **Source**: *British Indian Views of the Rauzāniyya Sect* (1994); *The Shias of Pakistan* (A. Rieck, 2015).

### 4. Durrani and Sikh Periods
- **Durrani Empire**: Orakzai tribes provided soldiers and negotiated autonomy with Ahmad Shah Durrani's empire, viewing themselves as part of the broader Afghan political world.
- **Sikh Expansion**: The Sikh Empire under Ranjit Singh attempted to control the frontier, leading to conflict and the construction of forts, though Orakzai territory remained largely autonomous.
- **Source**: *The Races of Afghanistan* (H.W. Bellew, 1880); *Afghan-Sikh Wars* (Wikipedia/Academic records).

## Evidence Hierarchy
- **A. Orakzai-Specific**: Lucas White King's monograph; Roshaniyya involvement in Tirah; Tirah Sayyid influence.
- **B. Pashtun-Wide**: Jirga as a customary institution; Pashtunwali principles; tribal confederation models.
- **C. Historical**: Mughal chronicles (*Akbarnama*); Durrani administrative records; British frontier reports.
- **D. Oral History**: Tribal origin legends; family memories of migration and resistance.

## Documented Terminology
- **Tirah**: The historical and geographic heart of Orakzai territory.
- **Roshaniyya**: The 16th-century socio-religious and political movement.
- **Masharan**: Tribal elders and leaders.
- **Jirga**: The customary deliberative assembly.
- **Khel**: A sub-clan or lineage section.
