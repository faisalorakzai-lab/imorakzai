# Research Notes: Page 74 — The Orakzai and the British Empire

## Core Concept
- **Imperial Encounter**: The relationship between the Orakzai and the British Empire was defined by a long and complex encounter at the frontier, involving military confrontation, political negotiation, and administrative adaptation.
- **Strategic Frontier**: The Orakzai homeland (Tirah) became strategically vital to British imperial defence due to its proximity to Afghanistan and its role as a buffer against Russian expansion (The Great Game).
- **Resistance and Resilience**: Orakzai communities responded to imperial expansion through various forms of resistance—from armed uprisings to non-cooperation—while also negotiating to protect their autonomy.

## Key Findings

### 1. Strategic Importance of the Frontier
- **The Great Game**: The northwestern frontier was the front line of British India's defence against potential Russian advancement through Central Asia and Afghanistan.
- **Mountain Passes**: Controlling the passes and maintaining stability in the tribal hills was essential for secure communication and trade routes.
- **Source**: *Geopolitics and Strategic Interests of the British in the Tribal Region* (R. Khan, 2020).

### 2. Forms of Orakzai Resistance
- **Armed Resistance**: Direct military confrontation during punitive expeditions (e.g., 1891, 1897).
- **Siege of Posts**: Attacking British signal posts and forts (e.g., Battle of Saragarhi).
- **Non-Cooperation**: Refusal to pay fines, surrender hostages, or allow road construction through tribal territory.
- **Collective Negotiation**: Using the Jirga to present a unified front against colonial demands.

### 3. Documented Military Expeditions
- **1855 Expedition**: Major-General Neville Chamberlain led a force against the Bizoti Orakzais following border raids.
- **1868-69 Expeditions**: Further punitive actions against the Bizoti section to enforce border security.
- **1891 Miranzai Expedition**: General Sir William Lockhart targeted Orakzai clans on the Samana Range to secure the ridge and build forts (Fort Lockhart, Fort Gulistan).
- **1897-98 Tirah Expedition**: The largest frontier campaign; triggered by a widespread uprising. 34,000 troops entered Tirah to subdue Orakzai and Afridi resistance.
- **Battle of Saragarhi (Sept 12, 1897)**: Orakzai and Afridi tribesmen attacked a British signal post defended by 21 Sikh soldiers. This event is a major marker of tribal resistance in the region.
- **Source**: *The Campaign in Tirah, 1897-1898* (H. D. Hutchinson, 1898).

### 4. Frontier Policy: Forward vs. Close Border
- **Close Border Policy (Pre-1870s)**: Managing the frontier from the plains with minimal intervention in the hills.
- **Forward Policy (Post-1870s)**: Active expansion into tribal territories, building forts, and establishing more direct political influence.
- **Negotiated Control**: A mixture of Political Agents, Maliks, and allowances used to manage autonomous zones.

## Evidence Hierarchy
- **A. Historical Records**: British military despatches (Lockhart, 1891, 1897), Parliamentary papers on the Tirah Campaign.
- **B. Contemporary Accounts**: H. D. Hutchinson (1898) and other war correspondents.
- **C. Academic Scholarship**: Sameetah Agha (2021) on the Tirah Campaign; Christian Tripodi (2013) on Political Officers.
- **D. Regional History**: KP provincial records and Pashtun frontier studies.

## Source Criticism & Terminology
- **Neutral Language**: Avoiding colonial terms like "fanatic" or "hostile" as objective facts; instead, describing them as colonial perceptions.
- **Orakzai Sections**: Distinguishing between specific clans (e.g., Bizoti, Alisherzai, Mamozai) rather than treating the tribe as a single monolith.
- **Contextual Omissions**: Recognizing that colonial reports often omitted the motivations for tribal resistance, such as the defense of land and autonomy.
