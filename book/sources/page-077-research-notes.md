# Research Notes: Page 77 — The Durand Line

## Core Concept
- **Negotiated Frontier**: The Durand Line is a 2,640-km (1,640 mi) boundary established in 1893 between British India and the Emirate of Afghanistan.
- **Spheres of Influence**: The 1893 agreement was primarily intended to define the respective "spheres of influence" of the two powers rather than a modern international border in the strict Westphalian sense.
- **Lived Borderland**: For tribes like the Orakzai, the line is a political reality that intersects with older, more fluid networks of kinship, trade, and migration.

## Key Findings

### 1. The 1893 Agreement
- **Date**: November 12, 1893.
- **Signatories**: Sir Henry Mortimer Durand (Foreign Secretary, British India) and Amir Abdur Rahman Khan (Emir of Afghanistan).
- **Structure**: A single-page document with seven short articles.
- **Key Provision**: Both parties agreed not to exercise interference in the territories lying beyond the line on the other's side.
- **Source**: *Actual Text of The Durand Line Agreement* (Afghanistan Online); *A History of Afghanistan* (Percy Sykes, 1940).

### 2. The Durand Commission (1894–1896)
- **Task**: To survey and demarcate the boundary on the ground wherever "practicable and desirable."
- **Execution**: Joint British-Afghan commissions worked in sections. 
- **Markers**: Approximately 258 boundary markers were placed along the frontier.
- **Key Figure**: Captain (later Sir) Henry McMahon led one of the most significant sections of the survey.
- **Source**: *Report of the Boundary Commission* (UNT Digital Library); *Britannica* (2026).

### 3. Reaffirmation (1919 & 1921)
- **1919 Treaty of Rawalpindi**: Following the Third Anglo-Afghan War, Article 5 explicitly accepted the frontier accepted by the late Amir.
- **1921 Treaty of Friendship**: Article 2 mutually accepted the frontier as defined in the 1919 treaty.
- **Significance**: These treaties are often cited by legal scholars as evidence of the boundary's settled status under international law.
- **Source**: *Encyclopaedia Iranica*; *EuroDocs* (BYU).

### 4. Orakzai and the Frontier
- **Geographic Nuance**: Orakzai is the only tribal agency/district that does not share a direct physical border with Afghanistan. It is landlocked by Kurram, Khyber, and Kohat.
- **Social Connectivity**: Despite the lack of a direct border, Orakzai tribes (e.g., Mamozai, Alikhel) have deep ties to the frontier. They use routes through Kurram and Khyber for trade and migration.
- **Smuggling & Trade**: The "smuggling of commodities" and "complex interplay of kinship" are central to the Orakzai economy, especially for sections living near the Kurram/Khyber borders.
- **Source**: *Pukhtun Economy and Society* (Akbar S. Ahmed, 1980); *Joshua Project* (Mamozai profile).

### 5. Contemporary Border Management
- **Fencing**: Pakistan began a massive fencing project in 2017 to secure the border.
- **Impact**: While aimed at security, the fence has significantly disrupted traditional cross-border movement for Pashtun tribes, turning "mountains into checkpoints."
- **Source**: *Politics Today* (2021); *EFSAS Study Paper*.

## Evidence Hierarchy
- **A. Primary Documents**: The 1893 Agreement text, the 1919 Treaty of Rawalpindi, and the 1921 Treaty of Friendship.
- **B. Administrative Records**: Durand Commission reports and boundary marker records.
- **C. Academic Scholarship**: Akbar S. Ahmed (ethnography), Percy Sykes (history), and international law reviews on boundary status.

## Source Criticism
- **Cartographic Bias**: Colonial maps often reflected administrative convenience rather than tribal realities.
- **Political Contestation**: Afghan and Pakistani official positions are divergent, requiring a neutral presentation of the "dispute" vs. "legal status."
- **Oral Memory Gap**: Specific Orakzai oral histories of the 1893 demarcation are sparse in published academic literature.
