ORAKZAI BEFORE BRITISH RULE

“Before the frontier was drawn on a modern map, communities already had histories of their own.”

Before the British frontier became a political system, Orakzai society already had its own geography, relationships, institutions and memories. The mountains were not an empty border. They were home. Communities were organized through kinship, villages, customary authority, religion, alliances and rivalries. Political power came from many directions. Empires expanded. Kings negotiated. Armies crossed frontiers. Tribes defended territory. But the existence of an empire nearby did not necessarily mean that every mountain community was directly governed by it. To understand Orakzai history, we therefore have to look beyond the modern map. The story begins with people, land and relationships. It continues through the Afghan, Mughal, Durrani and Sikh political worlds, and eventually reaches the period when British power began trying to impose a new frontier order.

What Does “Before British Rule” Mean?

Studying the period before formal British colonial administration requires examining multiple centuries and shifting political contexts. Orakzai society was shaped by successive regional empires, each interacting differently with the mountain tribes. Rather than a single static era, the pre-British period encompasses distinct phases of interaction with Mughal centralism, Durrani sovereignty, Sikh military expansion, and enduring tribal autonomy.

The Land Before the Modern District

Historical Orakzai territory cannot be simply equated with the boundaries of the modern administrative district established in 1973. Historically, the heartland of Tirah and the surrounding mountain passes functioned as an interconnected geographic and social sphere. The land served multiple vital purposes for the tribes: home, transit routes, economic resources, and defensive terrain.

“The multi-dimensional relationship between the Orakzai tribes and their historical territory.”

Tribal Organization and Clans

Historical records, most notably Lucas White King’s 1900 monograph, document the complex organization of the Orakzai tribe into major clans and sections. Comprising eighteen primary clans—including the Alisherzai, Mamozai, Mishti, and others—the tribe operated through nested levels of social structure: tribe, clan, section, local community, and family. This decentralized structure allowed local communities to maintain autonomy while uniting under broader tribal affiliation when faced with external pressure.1

The Jirga and Customary Authority

Governance in pre-British Orakzai society was anchored by the *Jirga*, a customary deliberative institution. Rather than relying on centralized state law, disputes were resolved, alliances formed, and community affairs decided through collective discussion and consensus among elders and leaders (*Maliks*). The Jirga represented a participatory system of conflict resolution embedded within the broader framework of Pashtun customary codes.

AUTHOR'S REFLECTION

History is often written by those who built empires, but the true story of the frontier is written in the endurance of the people who stayed. Before any foreign map was drawn across our mountains, our ancestors had already mastered the art of freedom. They negotiated with kings, defended their valleys, and governed themselves through the wisdom of the Jirga and the strength of kinship. The pre-British period was not an era of isolation; it was an era of self-reliance. To understand who we are today, we must remember the resilience of those who walked these mountain paths before us.

“AUTONOMY WAS NOT GRANTED; IT WAS DEFENDED.”
