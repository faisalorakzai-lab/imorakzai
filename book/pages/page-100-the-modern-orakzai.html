THE MODERN ORAKZAI

“Roots in the mountains. Life in a changing world.”

The modern Orakzai does not live outside history.

He or she carries history into a changing world.

The mountains remain. Families remain. Pashto remains part of many lives. Faith remains important. But the world around them has changed.

Children study in schools and universities. Young people use smartphones and digital platforms. Families live between villages and cities. Professionals work across Pakistan and beyond. Businesses operate through technology. Students learn skills that did not exist in their grandparents’ working lives. And families living overseas maintain connections across borders.

Modernity has not created a single new Orakzai identity. It has created new possibilities for living an old identity.

The question is no longer simply: “Where did we come from?” It is also: “What will we build from what we inherited?”

What does “modern” mean?

Modern does not simply mean urban, wealthy, Westernized, digital, or English-speaking. Modernity can include education, institutions, technology, mobility, new occupations, urban life, new communication, new political structures, new economic opportunities, and personal choices. A rural household using a mobile phone, a student moving between village and university, an elder using digital communication, and a professional returning with new knowledge can all be part of modern life.

Modern should not be treated as cultureless, and tradition should not be treated as the enemy of development. The more careful question is how people adapt, what they preserve, what they change, and what they decide to question.

From tribe to citizen

The 2018 Twenty-fifth Amendment formally brought the former FATA areas, including the former Orakzai Agency, into Khyber Pakhtunkhwa’s provincial framework.1 This is an administrative and constitutional change. It affects the formal setting of law, institutions, policing, courts, public administration, and citizenship. It does not by itself erase tribal identity, family identity, historical memory, language, religion, or cultural belonging.

Pages 73, 84, 85, and 86 described earlier administrative structures. Page 100 asks what happens when historic identities meet modern institutions. Administrative identity is not identical to cultural identity. The two may interact, and the relationship must be studied rather than assumed.

Education as transformation

Schools, colleges, universities, technical education, vocational education, digital learning, and professional education can expand knowledge, mobility, civic participation, and economic possibilities. They do not automatically produce employment or success. Higher education may lead into medicine, engineering, computer science, business, law, social sciences, education, agriculture, public administration, research, or other fields. No single field is inherently superior.

One possible pathway is village → school → college → university → profession → community. It is a possibility, not a universal sequence. Education can also take place through apprenticeships, religious learning, family knowledge, community work, technical training, and self-directed digital study.

Digital Orakzai

Smartphones, internet access, social media, online education, digital payments, remote work, freelancing, e-commerce, software, and digital communication are contemporary possibilities. They are not proof of universal access or district-wide adoption. Digital systems can widen opportunity while also exposing inequalities in connectivity, affordability, skills, language, privacy, and trust.

A 2025 UNDP/Japan-supported initiative trained 600 young people from Orakzai and Kurram in digital skills, with women comprising 52% of the programme participants.2 The evidence is concrete and important, but its scope is the programme cohort. It is not a statistic about all Orakzai youth, all Orakzai women, internet penetration, or employment.

Opportunity, not guarantee

Digital skills may create pathways toward freelancing, remote work, digital services, design, writing, marketing, consulting, software, or online education. Training is an opportunity, not a guarantee. Employment also depends on devices, electricity, internet quality, language, portfolios, clients, finance, safety, care responsibilities, market conditions, and institutional support.

Entrepreneurship can involve retail, agriculture, transport, hospitality, construction, technology, online commerce, professional services, or tourism. These are possible areas, not invented Orakzai startup statistics. Local knowledge plus digital tools can create new business possibilities, but the actual scale and outcomes require district-specific evidence.

Agriculture, trades, and markets

Agriculture can evolve without disappearing. Traditional knowledge may meet modern markets, irrigation, storage, transport, technology, and market access. Traditional trades can potentially adapt through branding, online sales, tourism, cooperatives, modern tools, and new markets. Pages 47, 49, and 50 provide the historical foundations for this discussion.

A conceptual commerce chain is local market + road + mobile phone + digital payment + online market = expanded commerce. The chain expresses a possibility. It does not claim that these systems already operate everywhere or that they produce equal benefit for every household.

Women and youth

Women in the modern Orakzai world should not be represented only through barriers. They may be students, teachers, doctors, entrepreneurs, professionals, mothers, researchers, artists, technology users, and community members; these roles are not mutually exclusive. The 2025 training initiative’s 52% women participation is evidence of inclusion in one cohort, not a district-wide women’s employment or education rate.2

Youth may connect education, digital skills, sport, career, and community. A 2025 District Youth Office career-counselling record documents themes including education, legal employment, digital skills, peacebuilding, and community development.3 It documents a public event and its agenda, not the aspirations of every young person.

Family, elders, and intergenerational learning

Families adapt to urbanization, migration, education, technology, work schedules, international travel, and digital communication. A traditional family network plus modern communication can become a connected family without assuming that every family is organized in the same way.

Elders should not be portrayed as opposed to modernity. They may carry history, moral guidance, family memory, language, and practical knowledge. Youth may bring technology, new skills, new ideas, education, and professional experience. When these flows move in both directions, dialogue becomes intergenerational learning rather than a contest between past and present.

Language and cultural preservation

Pashto, Urdu, English, and other languages can coexist in modern life. English or Urdu does not automatically replace Pashto, and speaking Pashto is not the only measure of cultural connection. Page 99 examined language, identity, and multilingualism; Page 100 extends that discussion into education, work, migration, and digital life.

Oral histories, photographs, music, proverbs, stories, family records, genealogy, and local vocabulary can be preserved through recordings, metadata, digital archives, and community-controlled collections. A digital archive should retain source, date, place, consent, and uncertainty. Heritage preservation is living work, not the freezing of culture unchanged.

AI and responsible digital heritage

AI may assist with translation, Pashto OCR, transcription, search, digital archiving, education, knowledge management, and cultural documentation. It must not invent genealogies, historical events, tribal traditions, festivals, family histories, songs, or cultural origins. AI should organize and assist human sources, not replace elders, community consent, historical criticism, or source verification.

The rule is AI + human sources = responsible digital heritage. When evidence is uncertain, the archive should say so. When a family record is private, it should not be published without permission. When a model generates a plausible sentence, plausibility is not proof.

Cities, Pakistan, and the world

Karachi, Peshawar, Islamabad, Lahore, and other cities can create new careers, networks, educational opportunities, lifestyles, and forms of community. Urbanization does not automatically eliminate cultural identity. Page 96 described Orakzai across Pakistan; Pages 97–99 traced overseas communities, global networks, and diaspora identity.

Orakzai people may participate in Pakistan’s wider national life through education, public service, business, professional careers, politics, arts, sports, science, technology, civil society, and community work. They may also build global professional lives. No unsupported achievement list or population claim is needed to recognize possibility and responsibility.

Citizenship and community development

Modern citizenship includes law, education, public institutions, civic participation, community service, rights, and responsibilities. It should not be reduced to party politics. Contemporary development priorities may include education, health, roads, connectivity, water, employment, digital access, women’s opportunities, youth skills, and the environment. These are analytical priorities, not an official Orakzai development plan.

Infrastructure affects participation in educational and economic systems. Roads, electricity, water, telecommunications, internet, schools, and health facilities matter, but district-specific quality and access require official measurement. Tourism can be a potential pathway through mountains, forests, valleys, cultural heritage, sporting events, and hospitality; it does not automatically produce prosperity.

Environment, health, and knowledge

Development and environment must be considered together. Forests, water, mountains, agriculture, tourism, and climate pressures are connected questions. No local climate statistic is invented here. Health modernization may involve access to healthcare, maternal and child health, health education, and telemedicine as a potential tool; no unsupported local outcome is stated.

The knowledge economy is an emerging possibility, not a claim that Orakzai has already become one. Land, labor, capital, knowledge, and technology can interact with coding, data, design, engineering, business, finance, healthcare, research, and digital communication. Teachers remain essential: technology can expand learning, but it does not replace a teacher.

Keep, change, rethink

Modernity invites three conversations. Some people may wish to keep language, family memory, hospitality, stories, heritage, dignity, and community responsibility. Some tools may change: technology, education, work, communication, and markets. Some practices may need to be rethought when they involve harm, exclusion, inequality, unsafe conditions, or unsupported stereotypes.

Cultural continuity does not require preserving every historical practice unchanged. Change does not require contempt for the past. The ethical task is to carry forward what sustains human dignity and to question what prevents people from learning, participating, or living safely.

The modern mindset and future

Learn, build, adapt, preserve, question, and contribute are possible values, not a definition of every Orakzai person. The future of work may include remote work, AI, automation, digital services, entrepreneurship, professional migration, and technical skills. The future of education may include online learning, hybrid education, digital libraries, AI-assisted learning, vocational training, and international education.

AI-assisted learning must include human teachers and source verification. Future cultural preservation should bring together elders, recording, digital archives, and youth. The future is not a guarantee. It is a responsibility shaped by choices, institutions, resources, and relationships.

Documented vs emerging

Documented: administrative change in 2018; the 2025 digital-skills programme; the 2025 career-counselling record; migration and urbanization as established book themes; and the existence of modern institutions and digital communication.

Emerging / potential: remote work, digital entrepreneurship, AI-assisted education, community-controlled digital archives, technology-driven agriculture, online tourism, and a knowledge economy. These are pathways to study and build, not current district-wide realities.

What still needs to be documented

Current youth aspirations; education outcomes; university participation; women’s employment and entrepreneurship; internet access; digital literacy; remote work; business ownership; migration and return migration; household economic change; agriculture modernization; healthcare access; infrastructure quality; language change; cultural preservation; social-media use; AI awareness; diaspora investment; tourism impact; environmental change; community organizations; and civic participation.

The modern Orakzai should be documented through evidence, not assumption. The future of the community cannot be understood only through its history; it must also be studied through the choices, aspirations, and everyday lives of people living it.

AUTHOR’S REFLECTION

The modern Orakzai is not a person who has left the past behind.

He is not a person trapped inside it either.

She may carry an old family name into a new profession. She may speak Pashto at home and another language at work. She may study in a university far from the mountains and still remember the village where her grandparents lived.

A young person may learn coding on a laptop while listening to stories from an elder. A farmer may use a smartphone to reach a market. A student may study abroad and return with new knowledge. A family may live in a city while keeping its connection to its ancestral homeland.

These are not contradictions. They are signs of a society changing.

The challenge is not to choose between tradition and modernity. The challenge is to understand what deserves to be carried forward, what deserves to be changed, and what deserves to be questioned.

The Orakzai story began long before the modern world. But it does not end there. The next chapter belongs to the people who are living it now.

THE FUTURE OF ORAKZAI
WILL NOT BE BUILT BY FORGETTING THE PAST.
IT WILL BE BUILT BY UNDERSTANDING IT.
