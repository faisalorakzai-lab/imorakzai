THE ORAKZAI AND THE BRITISH EMPIRE

“An empire met its limits at the frontier.”

An empire is often easiest to understand from its frontiers. At the centre, power can appear permanent. At the edge, power must negotiate with geography, distance, local authority and people who already possess their own political worlds. For the Orakzai, the arrival of the British Empire did not mean that one government simply replaced another overnight. It created a long encounter. There were military confrontations. There were negotiations. There were agreements. There were punishments. There were intermediaries. There were periods of relative calm. And there were moments when imperial policy collided directly with local autonomy. The history of the Orakzai and the British Empire is therefore not a simple story of conquest. It is a history of frontier power—and of a people negotiating, resisting and adapting to an expanding empire.

“A conceptual visualization of the imperial encounter: the approaching state meeting the mountain homeland.”

What was the British Empire?

The British Empire was a global imperial system, with British India serving as one of its most vital territories. The north-western frontier became strategically critical during the 19th century due to the geopolitical rivalry with the Russian Empire—known as the "Great Game." Afghanistan functioned as a buffer state, and the tribal territories, including those of the Orakzai, became the front line of imperial defence, trade routes, and security concerns.4

The Orakzai Homeland: Schematic Historical Map

To understand the encounter, we must look at the geography. Historically, the Orakzai territory centered around the mountain heartland of Tirah and the Samana Range. This terrain provided a natural fortress that protected tribal autonomy while placing the Orakzai at a strategic crossroads between the settled districts of Kohat and Peshawar and the routes to Afghanistan.

Forms of Resistance and Negotiation

Resistance to imperial expansion was multifaceted. While military confrontation is most frequently documented, resistance also took the form of non-cooperation, refusal to pay colonial fines, and collective negotiation through the Jirga. Conversely, periods of negotiation occurred when tribal sections sought to protect their interests or reached agreements regarding road safety and allowances. This dynamic relationship meant that authority was always a dialogue, never a simple decree.5

“The continuous tension between resistance and negotiation on the frontier.”

Documented Military Expeditions

When frontier policy shifted toward direct intervention (the "Forward Policy"), the British launched several punitive expeditions against Orakzai clans. These military actions were often the result of border disputes, raids on the plains, or the tribal refusal to accept colonial infrastructure like roads and forts. The Tirah Expedition (1897–98)

The Tirah Expedition was the most significant military encounter of the period. Triggered by a widespread tribal uprising in 1897, General Sir William Lockhart led a massive force of 34,000 troops into the heart of the Orakzai and Afridi homeland. The campaign featured fierce engagements, such as the **Storming of Dargai Heights** and the **Battle of Saragarhi**, where Orakzai and Afridi tribesmen besieged a British signal post. The expedition demonstrated the immense logistical challenges of mountain warfare and the deep-seated resistance of the tribes to foreign incursion.1 2

AUTHOR'S REFLECTION

The history of our encounter with the British Empire is etched into the very stones of the Samana Range. It was a time when the world’s greatest power met its limits in our valleys. While colonial records speak of 'pacification' and 'punishment,' our oral histories speak of the defense of home and the preservation of dignity. The Orakzai did not just survive this era; they navigated it with a strategic wisdom that allowed them to protect their identity while adapting to a changing world. We remember this history not to dwell on conflict, but to honor the resilience of those who stood firm in the face of an empire.

“POWER WAS MEASURED IN DISTANCE AND ENDURANCE.”
