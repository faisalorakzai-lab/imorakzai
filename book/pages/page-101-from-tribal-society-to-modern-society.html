FROM TRIBAL SOCIETY TO MODERN SOCIETY

“A society can change without forgetting where it came from.”

Logic Atlas: Social Transition

These 65 original SVG graphics translate the page’s reasoning into compact, printable structures. They distinguish documented administrative and development evidence from interpretive frameworks about continuity, adaptation, and social change.

Societies do not change in a single moment.

A road can change how a village connects to a market. A school can change how a child imagines a future. A university can change the professions available to a family. A smartphone can change how relatives communicate. Migration can place one family across several cities. A new law can change the institutions through which people interact with the state. And a new generation can change traditions simply by living them differently.

Orakzai society has experienced many such changes. The old structures did not disappear overnight. The new structures did not arrive complete. Between them exists a long period of adaptation.

This is the story of a society moving from one social framework toward another — while carrying memory, family, language, faith, and identity along the way.

What was “tribal society”?

The phrase must be used carefully. Traditional Orakzai and Pashtun community organization could include family networks, kinship, lineage, clan relationships, elders, jirga, hujra, hospitality, customary norms, religious institutions, local leadership, collective responsibility, and community cooperation. These were social structures, not a single identical system lived in exactly the same form by every person or village.

Historical community life contained cooperation and conflict, solidarity and hierarchy, mediation and competition, hospitality and inequality. The task is neither to romanticize nor condemn it. A careful account asks how particular institutions worked, for whom, under what conditions, and how their roles changed.

What is modern society?

Modern society can involve formal institutions, constitutional citizenship, education, urbanization, professional occupations, markets, technology, mass communication, mobility, formal law, public administration, and digital connectivity. It does not mean Westernization, the loss of Pashto, the loss of family, the loss of religion, or the loss of cultural identity.

Modernity is a social process, not a moral ranking. A community can have traditional family relationships, modern education, digital communication, customary practices, and formal legal institutions at the same time. Modernity is not the end of tradition; it is one way people adapt inherited lives to changing institutions and conditions.

Kinship and community

Kinship historically influenced identity, marriage, mutual support, social relationships, alliances, and community responsibilities. Pages 31–40 described tribe, lineage, family, and social structure. Page 101 adds a caution: kinship can shape the social field without determining every person’s modern choices.

Family → kinship → community is a useful model, not a universal rule. Education, migration, urbanization, employment, digital communication, and personal preference can widen a person’s relationships while family ties remain important.

Elders, jirga, and hujra

Elders could, often, or in some communities provide advice, mediation, memory, custom, and coordination. Not every elder possessed formal authority. A jirga can be understood as a customary deliberative institution in Pashtun social contexts, but jirgas are not identical everywhere and are not equivalent to Pakistan’s formal courts.

A hujra may serve hospitality, social interaction, community discussion, and cultural transmission. Not every Orakzai village maintained an identical hujra, and contemporary Orakzai research on how hujra institutions adapt to urbanization and digital communication remains limited. The responsible answer to “Did the hujra move into digital groups?” is: this requires evidence, not assumption.

Custom, religion, and state law

Customary norms, religious values, and state law have interacted in complex ways. Pashtun custom is not identical to Islamic law. Tribal custom is not identical to formal Pakistani law. Islamic law is not a synonym for either. The three categories may meet in lived practice, but they must be distinguished analytically and documented with care.

This distinction matters because a social practice may be described as customary without being treated as legally binding; a religious interpretation may influence community life without being identical to an ethnic tradition; and constitutional rights may provide standards that cannot be reduced to local custom.

From FCR to constitutional governance

Before the merger, former FATA operated through a distinct constitutional and administrative framework in which the Frontier Crimes Regulation was central. The Twenty-fifth Constitutional Amendment of 2018 brought the former FATA areas into Khyber Pakhtunkhwa.1 This created a new formal framework of provincial institutions, courts, public administration, and citizenship.

The reform was a major legal and administrative transformation, not the completion of social transformation. A constitutional change can establish new institutions while social adaptation continues for years. Pages 73–80, 85, 86, and 100 provide the historical path into this transition.

Agency to district

The administrative vocabulary changed from Orakzai Agency to Orakzai District. In analytical terms, agency → district and exceptional administration → provincial institutional framework describe a shift in formal governance. They do not describe every change in daily life, every public service, or every relationship between residents and institutions.

Modern society introduces additional institutional channels: district administration, courts, elected representatives, local government, schools, health services, and public offices. These channels do not automatically eliminate elders, jirga, hujra, family networks, or religious institutions. The question is how the channels interact, overlap, and sometimes conflict.

Family, marriage, birth, and mourning

Family life can adapt to education, migration, urbanization, employment, technology, work schedules, smaller households in some settings, and long-distance networks. It is not accurate to claim that all families have become nuclear. Marriage remains important while education, urban life, migration, and individual preferences may influence how families approach it; universal change cannot be claimed.

Birth and life-cycle traditions can continue while families use urban hospitals, modern healthcare, birth registration, education, and digital communication. Funeral gatherings, religious observance, community condolences, and family support can also connect village, city, and diaspora households. No Orakzai-specific statistic is invented.

Hospitality in modern life

Melmastia can survive in a village home, city apartment, restaurant, workplace, or diaspora household. The setting may change while the ethical meaning of welcoming and caring for a guest may continue in adapted form. This is an interpretive framework, not a claim that every modern encounter is identical to a traditional guest relationship.

Adaptation is often the mechanism through which traditions survive. A practice can change its venue, timing, tools, or participants without becoming meaningless. It can also be questioned when it creates harm or exclusion. Continuity should be described with “may continue,” not assumed for every person.

Education as the great transformer

Formal education can change knowledge, occupations, mobility, social networks, aspirations, women’s opportunities, political awareness, and economic possibilities. It does not guarantee employment. A possible pathway is home → school → college → university → profession, but apprenticeships, technical training, religious learning, family knowledge, and self-directed study also matter.

A 2020 UN Women report provides development context for women and girls in Orakzai, while a UNDP/UNICEF/KP education programme is documented in the project research as a targeted intervention in Kurram and Orakzai. Programme participation is not converted into district-wide outcomes.

Women and youth

Girls’ education, skills development, health, professional opportunities, and community participation are significant dimensions of social change. Women may be students, teachers, health professionals, entrepreneurs, workers, mothers, community members, and technology users. Modernization changes opportunities and expectations without reducing women to a development problem.

Youth increasingly interact with universities, digital platforms, professional networks, national institutions, and international opportunities. Tradition + education + technology can create new social possibilities. The 2024 vocational and 2025 digital-skills programmes are evidence of specific development initiatives, not district-wide employment or skills statistics.2 3

Digital and vocational transformation

Smartphones, internet, WhatsApp, social media, online education, digital payments, remote work, and digital services are contemporary possibilities with uneven access. In November 2024, a UNDP/Japan-supported programme reported 700 young participants from Kurram and Orakzai, including 375 women, trained in market-oriented vocational and technical skills such as mobile-phone repair, solar-related work, and other practical skills.2

In April 2025, UNDP reported 600 young people from Kurram and Orakzai trained in digital skills, with women comprising 52% of participants.3 Each number refers to a programme cohort. Neither is evidence that all Orakzai youth are digitally skilled, that 52% of Orakzai youth are in training, or that training automatically produces employment.

Economy, markets, migration, and cities

Economic life may combine agriculture, trade, construction, transport, public employment, professional services, technology, entrepreneurship, and remittances. No occupation is claimed to dominate. Roads, transport, mobile communication, digital payments, urban connections, and diaspora networks can expand market possibilities, but documented conditions must be separated from future potential.

Migration can transform family structures, occupations, education, language use, social networks, and economic expectations. Village ↔ city ↔ overseas is a connected pathway, not a one-way exit. Urban Orakzai life can involve different workplaces, schools, housing, social networks, and cultural environments while cultural identity continues. Urban life is not loss of identity.

Diaspora, language, and religion

Migration does not necessarily end community identity. Digital communication can support family contact, religious celebrations, financial support, cultural exchange, and oral-history preservation. Pages 90, 97, 98, 99, and 100 distinguish Orakzai-specific evidence from broader Pakistani, Pashtun, and diaspora context.

Pashto, Urdu, English, and other languages may be used differently at home, school, work, in media, and across locations. No disappearance of Pashto is predicted. Modernization also does not automatically mean secularization: people may participate simultaneously in religious life, modern education, professional work, digital society, and state institutions.

What changes, continues, and adapts?

Technology, education, work, mobility, law, and communication can change. Family, language, faith, memory, hospitality, and identity may continue. Marriage, family, hospitality, work, community, and cultural practice can adapt. These are analytical categories, not universal measurements.

Individual choice may expand in education, career, location, marriage, and lifestyle while family and community relationships remain important. Individualism has not simply replaced collectivism. A better model is community ↕ individual ↕ family.

Elders, youth, and community organizations

Social transition does not require one generation to defeat another. Elders may contribute memory and experience; youth may contribute skills and technology; dialogue can create intergenerational learning. Contemporary organizations may include schools, parent groups, NGOs, youth groups, professional associations, sports organizations, and community development groups. Their reach and impact require local documentation.

The 2022 UNDP/Japan programme for merged districts focused on stabilization, economic recovery, social cohesion, local governance, and livelihood opportunities, with planned employment opportunities for 1,300 youth through digital skills, incubation, and skills training.4 It is a contemporary development intervention, not proof that it transformed all of Orakzai society.

Law, rights, and citizenship

Modern civic life can be represented as custom + law + rights + institutions. Constitutional rights, legal identity, political representation, public services, and civic responsibilities form a multi-layered citizenship. Tribal identification, district administration, provincial citizenship, and national citizenship can coexist. The transition from exceptional arrangements toward constitutional governance is historically significant, but it should not be declared fully complete without evidence.

Human dignity and equal rights provide an ethical boundary. Cultural heritage should not be used to justify harm, discrimination, exclusion, unsafe practice, collective punishment, or denial of individual rights. Reform can be compatible with cultural dignity when it protects people rather than attacking their identity.

Modern society is not perfect

Modern society can create opportunity, mobility, education, rights, and technology while also producing inequality, unemployment, migration pressure, infrastructure gaps, digital divides, education gaps, institutional weakness, environmental pressure, isolation, misinformation, cultural fragmentation, and urban stress. These are challenges to document, not local statistics to invent.

Traditional society was not simple, and modern society is not simple. Both can contain cooperation and conflict, solidarity and inequality, belonging and pressure. The balanced model is past + present + future, followed by remember + adapt + build.

Modernity and cultural identity

A society does not lose its identity simply because its institutions change.

Identity can survive in language. It can survive in memory. It can survive in family relationships. It can survive in stories. It can survive in faith. It can survive in the choices people make about what they carry forward.

Preservation may prioritize language, oral histories, family memory, historical records, hospitality, poetry, music, traditional knowledge, and community solidarity. Reform may address unsafe practices, discrimination, exclusion, barriers to education, harmful stereotypes, collective punishment, and denial of individual rights. Not every historical practice should remain unchanged.

Documented vs interpretive

Documented: the 2018 constitutional merger; formal administrative change; targeted education and development initiatives; the 2024 vocational programme; the 2025 digital-skills programme; migration and urbanization as established book themes; and the existence of modern institutions.

Interpretive / conceptual: identity adaptation; intergenerational negotiation; changing family expectations; tradition-modernity coexistence; the possibility of a modern hujra; and future cultural preservation. Interpretation is labelled as interpretation. It is not presented as a measured district-wide fact.

What still needs to be documented

Village-level social change; the changing role of jirga; the changing role of hujra; family structure; marriage decisions; women’s experiences; youth aspirations; education outcomes; professional mobility; digital access and behaviour; migration and return migration; diaspora relationships; language change; religious practice; customary law; formal court use; community organizations; economic transformation; entrepreneurship; agricultural modernization; urban identity; and intergenerational relationships.

From tribal society to modern society is not a single historical event. It is an ongoing social process that requires continued fieldwork, oral history, demographic research, and community-based documentation.

AUTHOR’S REFLECTION

Every generation believes it is living through an unprecedented change.

Perhaps every generation is partly right.

The Orakzai world I inherit contains many worlds at once.

There is the memory of elders. There is the language of the home. There are mountains, villages, and family histories. There are schools, universities, and professional careers. There are cities, migration routes, and overseas communities. There are smartphones, digital platforms, and new forms of work.

None of these worlds exists completely alone. They overlap.

The modern Orakzai therefore cannot be understood by looking only at the past or only at the present. We have to look at the connection between them.

A society becomes modern not when it forgets its roots, but when it learns how to carry those roots into institutions, education, technology, and a changing world.

The responsibility of my generation is not simply to inherit a culture. It is to understand it. To preserve what gives dignity. To question what causes harm. To learn what the future requires. And to leave the next generation something stronger than what we received.

MODERNITY IS NOT THE END OF TRADITION.
IT IS THE CONTINUING STORY OF HOW PEOPLE ADAPT.
