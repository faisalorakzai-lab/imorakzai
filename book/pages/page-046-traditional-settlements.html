TRADITIONAL SETTLEMENTS

“Where landscape became home.”

Before roads connected distant towns, before concrete became the dominant building material, settlement followed the landscape. People built where the terrain allowed. They looked for access to water, workable land, routes and protection. A settlement was not simply a collection of houses. It was a system.

Continuing the geographical sequence from mountains, valleys, water, climate, and environment established in preceding chapters, the built environment of Orakzai represents an intricate adaptation to topography. Traditional settlements emerged as functional responses to the constraints and opportunities of a rugged mountain terrain.12

“Structural hierarchy from mountain ridge and valley watercourse to fields, houses, and community space.”

What Is a Traditional Settlement?

The term "traditional settlement" does not necessarily mean ancient. It refers to patterns of settlement and built environment that developed before, or alongside, modern urban infrastructure and construction systems. These patterns reflect collective knowledge, material availability, and social organization refined over generations.23

“Synthesis of tradition, landscape, and local needs into settlement patterns.”

Landscape & Water First

Mountainous terrain strictly circumscribes where permanent habitation can be established. Settlements naturally gravitate toward valley floors, terraced slopes, and areas within reach of perennial watercourses like the Mastura River and Khanki Toi. Proximity to water ensures household survival, livestock maintenance, and agricultural irrigation.14

“Progression from mountain slope and terrace to permanent settlement.”

Settlement Density & Clustered Communities

Settlement density in Orakzai varies widely depending on local terrain, agricultural capacity, and kinship ties. While some households occupy isolated positions near fields, many traditional habitations are clustered. Clustering allowed families belonging to the same *Khel* (clan) to share resources, maintain collective security, and preserve arable land for cultivation.25

“Spectrum of settlement density from dispersed homesteads to clustered community cores.”

Vernacular Architecture: Stone, Mud & Timber

Architectural surveys in the wider Tirah and Orakzai region indicate that traditional residential construction relies heavily on local materials. Approximately 49% of traditional structures utilize stone masonry and mud, while others incorporate compacted earth and timber for ceilings, doors, and window frames, offering insulation against harsh winter conditions.34

Architectural Element Traditional Material Functional Purpose
Walls Stone masonry and mud mortar [3] Structural strength, thermal mass, defensive barrier
Ceilings & Roofs Timber beams, brushwood, and compacted earth [4] Insulation against winter cold; weight distribution
Courtyards Open walled enclosure Private family space, light, ventilation, domestic work
Apertures Narrow wooden doors and small windows Climate defense, security, and privacy control

The Residential Compound & Communal Spaces

The fundamental unit of Orakzai residential life is the compound—a walled enclosure (*Kala*) that organizes living quarters, storage for grain and fodder, and livestock housing around a central courtyard. Beyond individual homes, settlements incorporate pivotal communal institutions: the **Hujra** (male guest house and community center) and the **Mosque**, which serve as the anchors of social and spiritual life.25

“Organizational scale from individual households to compounds, public spaces, and community.”

AUTHOR'S REFLECTION

A house in Orakzai was never just four walls of stone and mud. It was built to lean against the mountain, to shelter against the winter frost, and to open its doors to kin and traveler alike. The stones we stacked came from the very slopes we walked upon. In every wall and every courtyard, our ancestors inscribed an unbreakable contract with the land: we live where the mountain permits, and we build to endure.

“The stone of the mountain became the walls of the home.”
