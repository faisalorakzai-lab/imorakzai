ORAKZAI SUB-TRIBES

“Sections, clans and the structure of a tribal community.”

Tribe Sections Clans Lineages Families

The name Orakzai describes a wider tribal community, but within that community are many smaller identities. Families identify themselves through sections, clans, lineages, villages, or ancestral names.

Historical and administrative records reveal a complex internal structure rather than a single simple hierarchy. Terms have also been recorded differently across periods and sources by chroniclers and administrators such as Lucas White King in his 1900 monograph on the Orakzai. Among the numerous sections documented in historical literature, two major branches stand out prominently by virtue of their demographic size, territorial scope, and historical influence: the **Ali Khel** and the **Mamozai**.123

“The nested structural hierarchy of Orakzai tribal organization.”

Prominent Sections: Ali Khel and Mamozai

Within the broad spectrum of Orakzai tribal organization, historical surveys and contemporary ethnographic data identify the **Ali Khel** and the **Mamozai** as two of the largest and most influential sections. The Ali Khel, traditionally occupying extensive territories in Upper Orakzai, have historically formed major jirgas and community leadership networks. Similarly, the Mamozai—who form a cornerstone of Upper Orakzai tribal structure and represent the author's own ancestral lineage—play a vital role in regional governance, social cohesion, and cultural preservation.34

Historical Sources and Terminology

British administrative records—most notably Sir Lucas White King's 1900 Monograph on the Orakzai Country and Clans—provide detailed mappings of these groups, distinguishing between core tribal sections and associated communities (ham-saya groups such as the Mishti and Sheikhan). However, historical classifications must be interpreted as administrative and ethnographic snapshots rather than rigid corporate structures.15

AUTHOR'S REFLECTION

When I hear an Orakzai introduce himself through a smaller tribal or family identity—whether as Mamozai, Ali Khel, or another historic section—I do not hear a division from the wider tribe. I hear another layer of the same history. A name tells us where a family remembers its roots, which community it belongs to, which relationships shaped its past, and how that identity was carried forward. The larger name is Orakzai. Inside it are many smaller histories. Understanding those histories does not divide the story; it makes the story deeper.

“One tribe. Many names. Generations of memory.”
